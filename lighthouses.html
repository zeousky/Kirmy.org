lighthouses

architecture dedicated to yelling 'rock here' at ships

Lighthouses are navigation aids built to make dangerous coastlines, harbor entrances, reefs, and other hazards visible from far away. Their entire job is turning one fixed point into an unmistakable signal, often in weather specifically designed to make seeing difficult.

why height matters

Light travels in roughly straight lines, while Earth's surface curves away. Raising a light increases the distance from which it can be seen.

That is why towers on low coastlines may be tall, while lights on high cliffs can use much shorter structures.

making a light visible

Early lighthouses used fires, candles, and oil lamps. Later systems used powerful lamps, reflectors, and lenses to concentrate light into useful beams.

Fresnel lenses were especially important because they could focus large amounts of light while using far less material than a conventional thick lens.

identifying the lighthouse

Different lights use distinct flash patterns, colors, or sectors so mariners can identify them on charts.

A repeating pattern might involve several short flashes followed by a pause. At sea, apparently even architecture needs a username.

automation changed the job

Many lighthouses once required resident keepers to maintain lamps, lenses, fuel, fog signals, and buildings.

Modern navigation systems, reliable electric equipment, and automation eliminated the need for permanent staff at most sites.

small facts
  • Fresnel lenses use concentric sections to reduce weight.
  • Some lighthouses also use foghorns or other sound signals.
  • Many historic lighthouse towers are still active navigation aids.
  • Modern ships also rely on GPS, radar, charts, and electronic navigation systems.
