maps are organized lies

projection, scale, symbols, and the impossible task of flattening a planet

Every flat map distorts reality. Earth is roughly spherical, paper and screens are flat, and geometry refuses to negotiate. A map therefore chooses what to preserve and what to sacrifice.

projection is compromise

A map projection transforms coordinates from a curved surface onto a plane.

No projection can perfectly preserve area, shape, distance, and direction everywhere at once. Improving one property usually makes another worse.

the mercator problem

The Mercator projection preserves local angles, which historically made it valuable for navigation.

But area distortion grows dramatically toward the poles, making high-latitude regions appear much larger than they really are.

symbols matter

Maps simplify. Roads become lines, cities become dots, elevation becomes color or contour, and political boundaries become strokes that often look much more definite than they are in real life.

Every map is therefore an argument about what information deserves attention.

digital maps still choose

Interactive maps can zoom, rotate, update, and layer data, but they still depend on projection choices, data quality, labeling rules, and editorial priorities.

Putting a map on a phone did not remove bias. It merely added a blue dot and battery anxiety.

small facts
  • Different projections are designed for different purposes.
  • A map's scale determines how much detail can be represented.
  • Contour lines connect points of equal elevation.
  • North does not have to be at the top of a map.
