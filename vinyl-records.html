vinyl records

music stored as microscopic wiggles in plastic

A vinyl record stores sound as a physical groove. A stylus traces that groove, tiny movements become an electrical signal, and speakers turn the signal back into sound. The whole process is absurdly mechanical and somehow still works beautifully.

the groove contains the signal

During mastering, audio is converted into a continuously varying groove cut into a lacquer or other master medium.

For stereo records, the two channel signals are encoded in different directions of groove movement.

the stylus reads it

As the record rotates, the stylus follows the groove and vibrates.

A cartridge converts those mechanical movements into a small electrical signal, which is then amplified and sent to speakers.

why records have limits

Bass uses more groove space, very loud passages can be difficult to cut, and the available space decreases toward the center of the record.

Engineers account for these constraints during mastering. Physical media has opinions.

why vinyl returned

Streaming is vastly more convenient, but records provide large artwork, physical ownership, deliberate sequencing, and the ritual of handling an album.

For many listeners, the appeal is not that vinyl is objectively superior in every technical sense. It is that listening becomes an activity rather than background access to an infinite catalog.

small facts
  • Most modern LPs rotate at 33⅓ rpm.
  • Singles commonly use 45 rpm.
  • Dust and scratches can create audible clicks and pops.
  • A phono preamp applies gain and equalization before normal amplification.
